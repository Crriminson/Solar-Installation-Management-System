## Inverter fault codes differ by brand
The same code can mean different things on different brands and even different models. Always look up the code in that inverter's manual. Record the exact code and a photo of the display on the ticket. The assistant only covers the common kinds of fault, not specific codes.
Source: https://www.solaxpower.com/blogs/solar-inverter-fault-codes.html

## Isolation fault or earth fault on the inverter
This means the inverter has detected low insulation resistance between the DC side and earth, so current may be leaking. Common causes are damaged PV cable insulation or water inside a DC connector, often after rain. Treat it as a safety problem. Do not bypass it or reset it repeatedly. Isolate the system, disconnect the strings and test each string's insulation to earth to find the faulty one. Inspect cables where they cross edges, clamps and connectors. Keep the faulty string disconnected until it is repaired.
Source: https://www.solaxpower.com/blogs/solar-inverter-fault-codes.html

## Grid overvoltage fault on the inverter
The inverter trips when grid voltage goes above its allowed range. This is usually a grid problem, not an inverter fault, and is common at midday when many nearby homes export. Measure the AC voltage at the inverter terminals while it is running. A thin or long AC cable can add to the rise, so check its voltage drop as well. If it happens often, raise it with the DISCOM. Do not change inverter grid settings without Operations approval.
Source: https://www.solaxpower.com/blogs/solar-inverter-fault-codes.html

## Inverter is off with no generation
Check in this order: is there grid power at the site (an on-grid inverter shuts down when the grid is off), is the ACDB MCB on, is the DC isolator on, is it daylight with enough sun, what does the display say. Then measure DC voltage at the input. Zero volts from a string points to an open connection, a blown fuse or a disconnected connector. Check the DCDB fuses before opening anything on the roof.

## Low generation with no fault code
Compare with about 4 units per kW per day, adjusted for season. Then check:
- Dirty panels: clean them and compare the next day.
- New shade: tree growth, a new water tank or a neighbour's building.
- One string low: compare string voltages and currents; a lower voltage suggests a failed bypass diode or a panel problem.
- A loose or hot connector.
- Inverter clipping or heat derating at midday.
- Grid trips: check the inverter's fault history.
Record the readings on the ticket.

## DC overvoltage fault
This means the string voltage is above the inverter's limit, usually from too many panels in series, often seen on cold mornings. Check the string's Voc against the inverter's maximum DC voltage. Disconnect the string and report it. Do not leave it connected, because overvoltage can destroy the inverter and is unlikely to be covered by warranty.

## How to clean solar panels
Clean with plain water and a soft cloth or sponge, early in the morning or in the evening when the panels are cool. Do not use harsh chemicals, abrasive pads or high-pressure jets, and do not spray cold water on hot glass. Do not stand on panels. How often depends on dust, and in most places every two to four weeks is reasonable. Monsoon rain helps, but does not replace cleaning entirely.

## Common signs of a bad installation on a service visit
Look for: browned or melted connectors, mixed connector brands, cable touching the roof or sharp edges, loose earth bonding, rusted clamps, water in the DCDB, a tripped SPD indicator, hot spots or cracked glass on a panel, and an inverter in direct sun. Photograph anything you find and put it on the ticket, because warranty assessment depends on it.
